On 16 years nobody had looked at (1989–2004), with the exact same rules, **C5 held up and C4 did not.**

- **C5 (trend + credit) is VALIDATED.** It grew 15.4% a year against the S&P 500's 12.2% ($100,000 → $0.99M vs $0.63M). Its worst
  drawdown was 35.8%, against the index's 47.4%. It lost the 1989–94 sub-period and won 1995–99 and 2000–04. Through the dot-com bust
  (March 2000 to October 2002) it lost 7.8% while the index lost 32.8%.
- **C4 (credit-filtered 2x) is NOT VALIDATED, and the failure matters.** It grew 11.7% a year, slower than the index. Its worst drawdown
  was 56.6%, deeper than the index's, and it won only 2000–04. The credit signal was excellent in credit-driven stress: it was already in
  T-bills before the 1990 recession and before the 1998 LTCM crisis, and was flat in both. It was wrong in the dot-com bust. That was a
  stock-valuation collapse, not a credit crisis, so spreads looked "calm" in May 2001 and January 2002. Each time it went back into 2x
  S&P straight into the next leg down (−19.6% in 2001, −28.7% in 2002). **A credit filter alone only protects you from credit crises.**
  The price trend in C5 is what caught 2000–02.
- **C1 (reference, no verdict)** was strongest on return (17.4% a year), but it lost 33% in 1990 and more than C5 in both short crises.

**What this means.** Across all 37 years (stitched, descriptive only), C5 grew 14.4% a year against the S&P 500's 11.3%. Its worst
drawdown was 38.8% versus 55.2%, and it beat the index in both independent tests. That is the most robust result in this research so
far. C4, the candidate the 2005–2025 rules sent to the holdout, failed the test that mattered most.
